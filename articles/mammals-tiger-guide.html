Tiger: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Panthera tigris

Tiger is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

Forests, grasslands, mangroves and other habitats across parts of South and East Asia, depending on subspecies and population. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Deer, wild pigs and other medium-to-large animals; prey choice depends on the ecosystem. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

Tigers are generally solitary and maintain territories that may overlap with those of females. They use scent marks, scratches and vocalisations to communicate. A tiger's home range can be large, especially where prey is scarce. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

Dark stripes break up the outline of the body in vegetation. Strong limbs support stalking and short bursts of power, while padded paws help the animal move quietly. Each tiger's stripe pattern is distinctive. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

A female usually gives birth to a small litter after a gestation of roughly three and a half months. Cubs remain dependent on their mother while learning to hunt and navigate their territory. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Major pressures include habitat loss and fragmentation, poaching, depletion of prey and conflict near settlements. Protected landscapes connected by wildlife corridors are important for long-term survival. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Never seek out a tiger on foot or use bait to attract one. Keep a respectful distance and follow local wildlife authorities. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.

Learning note: This is an educational species profile, not veterinary, wildlife-handling or field-identification advice. Confirm local regulations and current scientific assessments when using this information for research.
