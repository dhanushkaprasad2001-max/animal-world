Leatherback Sea Turtle: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Dermochelys coriacea

Leatherback Sea Turtle is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

Open oceans worldwide, migrating between feeding grounds and tropical or subtropical nesting beaches. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Mostly jellyfish and other soft-bodied gelatinous animals. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

Leatherbacks are the largest living turtles and travel vast distances between feeding and nesting areas. Unlike hard-shelled sea turtles, they have a flexible, leathery carapace with ridges. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

A streamlined body and long front flippers support long-distance swimming and deep dives. Their unusual body covering and internal heat-retention mechanisms help them forage in cooler waters than many other sea turtles. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

Females return to sandy beaches to lay eggs in nests dug above the tide line. Hatchlings emerge together and move towards the sea, although many face predators and artificial lighting. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Threats include accidental capture in fishing gear, plastic ingestion, egg collection, coastal development and artificial lights that disorient hatchlings. The global population is Vulnerable, with some regional populations in worse condition. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Keep beaches dark and clean, never touch hatchlings, and report injured turtles to authorised rescue organisations. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.

Learning note: This is an educational species profile, not veterinary, wildlife-handling or field-identification advice. Confirm local regulations and current scientific assessments when using this information for research.
