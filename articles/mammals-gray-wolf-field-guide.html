Gray Wolf: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

The gray wolf is a large, social canid with a wide historical range across the Northern Hemisphere. It lives in habitats ranging from tundra and boreal forest to mountains and temperate woodland, where suitable prey and space remain.

Appearance and identification

Wolves vary greatly in size and coat colour across their range. They have long legs, a deep chest, large paws and strong jaws. Their appearance overlaps with some domestic dogs, so identification should consider the full animal and location.

Habitat and distribution

The gray wolf is a large, social canid with a wide historical range across the Northern Hemisphere. It lives in habitats ranging from tundra and boreal forest to mountains and temperate woodland, where suitable prey and space remain.

Diet and feeding behaviour

Wolves are carnivores that commonly hunt hoofed mammals such as deer, elk, moose or wild boar, depending on region. They also take smaller prey and scavenge when opportunities arise.

Social behaviour and reproduction

A wolf pack is usually a family group, often consisting of a breeding pair and offspring from one or more years. How large packs become and how they hunt depend on prey, habitat and human pressures.

Ecological role and conservation

Wolves can influence prey behaviour and food webs, but coexistence challenges include livestock losses and competition concerns. Effective conservation combines habitat connectivity, monitoring, livestock protection and locally appropriate conflict-prevention measures.

Observing wild canids responsibly

Observe wild animals from a respectful distance, never feed or attempt to touch them, keep pets under control, and follow local wildlife guidance. Photographs should never be worth disturbing an animal or approaching a den.

Scientific name: Canis lupus. Common names and taxonomic classifications can differ among authorities; consult current regional field guides when making formal identifications.
