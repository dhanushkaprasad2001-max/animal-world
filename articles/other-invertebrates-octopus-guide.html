Other Invertebrates Explained: The Intelligent Octopus

Invertebrates are animals without a backbone. They make up the vast majority of described animal species and include insects, spiders, crustaceans, molluscs, worms, corals, jellyfish and sea stars. Their body plans range from microscopic animals to enormous ocean-dwelling species.

The octopus: a mollusc without a shell

Octopuses are cephalopod molluscs. Unlike many familiar molluscs, they do not have an external shell. Their soft bodies, large heads and eight flexible arms make them highly distinctive. The giant Pacific octopus (Enteroctopus dofleini) is among the largest octopus species.

Eight arms and remarkable control

Each arm contains many suckers that can grip, taste and explore surfaces. A large part of an octopus's nervous system is distributed through its arms, allowing complex movements and local sensory responses. Octopuses can squeeze through surprisingly small openings when the hard beak can fit through.

Camouflage and defence

Specialised skin cells allow many octopuses to change colour and pattern rapidly. Some can also alter skin texture to resemble rocks, algae or coral. Camouflage helps them avoid predators and approach prey. When threatened, an octopus may release ink and use jet propulsion to escape.

Learning and behaviour

Octopuses show impressive problem-solving abilities, exploration and learning in laboratory and natural settings. They may open containers, navigate complex spaces and manipulate objects. Their intelligence is different from that of mammals and birds, and scientists continue to study how cephalopod nervous systems support behaviour.

Life cycle

Most octopus species have relatively short lives compared with many vertebrates. After mating, females of many species guard their eggs and may stop feeding while caring for them. The female usually dies around the time the eggs hatch. Reproductive strategies vary among species.

Protecting marine invertebrates

Octopuses and other invertebrates depend on healthy marine ecosystems. Pollution, habitat destruction, destructive fishing practices and climate change can affect their survival. Protecting rocky reefs, seagrass beds and other coastal habitats helps many invertebrate groups.
