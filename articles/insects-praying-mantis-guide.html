European Mantis: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Mantis religiosa

European Mantis is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

Grasslands, scrub, gardens and other warm habitats across parts of Europe, Africa and Asia; introduced populations occur elsewhere. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Other insects and small arthropods captured with the front legs. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

A praying mantis is an ambush predator that often remains still among vegetation before striking. Its head can rotate, giving it a wide field of view. Not all mantis species show the same behaviours or distribution. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

The front legs are modified into spiny grasping limbs that hold prey. Camouflage helps the mantis blend into leaves and stems, while large compound eyes help detect movement. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

Females lay eggs inside a protective foamy case called an ootheca. Young mantises hatch as nymphs and grow through a series of moults before becoming adults. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Habitat simplification and pesticide use can reduce mantis prey and suitable shelter. Some species are also affected by collection for the pet trade. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Observe mantises without handling them and avoid pesticide spraying where beneficial insects are active. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.
