Komodo Dragon: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Varanus komodoensis

Komodo Dragon is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

A small group of Indonesian islands, including Komodo, Rinca, Flores and nearby islands. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Carnivore and scavenger; prey includes deer, pigs and smaller animals. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

Komodo dragons are the largest living lizards. They are generally solitary but may gather around large carcasses. They use their forked tongues to collect scent particles and locate food across their island habitats. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

A muscular body, strong limbs, sharp claws and serrated teeth help subdue prey. Their saliva contains many microbes, but research also identifies venom-related glands; prey capture is more complex than the old bacteria-only explanation. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

Females lay eggs in nests, including excavated mounds or abandoned megapode nests. Hatchlings are vulnerable and often spend time in trees, where they can avoid larger dragons. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

The species is listed as Endangered. Habitat changes, human activity, reduced prey and climate-related impacts threaten its restricted range. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Visit only with authorised park guides, remain at the required distance and never attempt to feed or approach a dragon. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.
