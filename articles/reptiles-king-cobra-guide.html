King Cobra: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Ophiophagus hannah

King Cobra is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

Forests, bamboo thickets, mangroves and other habitats across parts of South and Southeast Asia. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Primarily other snakes, including venomous and non-venomous species. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

The king cobra is the world's longest venomous snake. It is usually shy and avoids people when given an escape route. During the breeding season, a female may construct a nest from leaf litter, an unusual behaviour among snakes. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

When threatened, it can raise the front part of its body and spread a hood. Its venom affects the nervous system and can be medically dangerous. Excellent chemical sensing through the tongue helps it track prey. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

The female lays eggs in a leaf-litter nest and may guard the nest for a period. Young snakes hatch fully independent and possess venom from birth. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Forest loss, persecution, road mortality and illegal wildlife trade are important threats. Conservation depends on habitat protection and reducing snake killing through education. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Never handle or provoke a king cobra. If encountered, retreat slowly and contact trained local wildlife responders. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.

Learning note: This is an educational species profile, not veterinary, wildlife-handling or field-identification advice. Confirm local regulations and current scientific assessments when using this information for research.
