Golden Poison Frog: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Phyllobates terribilis

Golden Poison Frog is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

Humid lowland rainforest in a small region of the Pacific coast of Colombia. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Small invertebrates, especially ants, termites and other tiny arthropods. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

The golden poison frog is famous for potent skin toxins. In the wild, these toxins are associated with chemicals obtained from its diet; captive-raised frogs generally do not develop the same toxicity when those prey compounds are absent. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

Bright yellow, orange or greenish coloration warns predators that the frog is chemically defended. Its small size and moist skin reflect the needs of amphibians living in humid environments. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

Females lay eggs in moist terrestrial sites. After hatching, a parent may carry tadpoles on its back to small pools of water where they continue development. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Deforestation, habitat fragmentation and illegal collection threaten this species. Its restricted range makes the protection of remaining rainforest especially important. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Never touch wild poison frogs or attempt to collect them. Observe them only from a respectful distance. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.
