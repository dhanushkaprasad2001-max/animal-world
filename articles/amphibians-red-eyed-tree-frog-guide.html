Amphibians Explained: The Red-Eyed Tree Frog

Amphibians are vertebrates that typically have moist, permeable skin and life cycles that involve water. Frogs, toads, salamanders, newts and caecilians belong to this group. Many amphibians lay eggs in water or damp places, and their young often develop through a larval stage before becoming adults.

The red-eyed tree frog

The red-eyed tree frog (Agalychnis callidryas) lives in humid lowland forests of Central America. Its bright green body, red eyes, blue-and-yellow sides and orange toes make it easy to recognise. It is a nocturnal species that spends much of the day resting on leaves.

Life in the canopy

Large adhesive toe pads help the frog cling to leaves and branches. Its green colour can blend with foliage, while sudden flashes of bright colour may startle a predator long enough for the frog to escape. It is a small insect-eating amphibian and helps form part of the rainforest food web.

Eggs and tadpoles

Females usually lay clusters of eggs on leaves overhanging water. When the eggs hatch, tadpoles drop into the water below and continue their development there. This unusual strategy links the forest canopy with ponds and temporary pools.

Why amphibian skin matters

Amphibian skin can absorb water and exchange gases, making many species sensitive to pollution, chemicals, drying and changes in temperature. Their dependence on both suitable land and aquatic habitats makes them useful indicators of ecosystem health.

Threats and protection

Habitat loss, disease such as chytrid fungal infections, pollution and climate change have contributed to amphibian declines worldwide. Protecting forest cover, clean freshwater, breeding ponds and connected habitats can benefit frogs and many other species.

Responsible observation

Observe frogs without handling them. Oils, soaps, insect repellents and pathogens on human hands can harm amphibians. Do not move frogs or tadpoles between ponds, because this can spread disease and disrupt local populations.

Field note: Amphibians are not simply “small reptiles.” Their permeable skin and life-cycle adaptations make them a distinct vertebrate group.
