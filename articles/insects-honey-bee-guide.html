Western Honey Bee: Habitat, Diet, Behaviour and Conservation

Scientific name: Apis mellifera

Western Honey Bee is one of the many animals that make the natural world diverse. This field guide summarises its ecology and the conditions it needs to survive. Common names and classification can vary across regions and may change as scientific research develops.

Habitat and distribution

A wide range of habitats where flowering plants are available; native to parts of Europe, Africa and western Asia and introduced elsewhere. Habitat quality, seasonal conditions and access to food, water or breeding sites influence where individuals can survive. Populations may be separated by natural barriers or by human changes to the landscape.

Diet and feeding

Nectar for energy and pollen as a source of protein and other nutrients. Feeding behaviour is closely linked to anatomy and habitat. The availability of appropriate food can change with seasons, weather and the condition of the ecosystem. A balanced food web is therefore important for the long-term health of wild populations.

Behaviour and social life

Honey bees are social insects living in colonies with a queen, workers and seasonal drones. Workers perform different tasks as they age, including cleaning, nursing larvae, building comb, guarding and foraging. Behaviour is not identical in every individual. Age, sex, season, local conditions and disturbance can all affect how an animal moves, communicates, finds food and interacts with others.

Physical adaptations

Their branched body hairs collect pollen, and specialised structures on the hind legs help carry it. Bees communicate food locations through movement patterns known as the waggle dance. Adaptations are features shaped over many generations that can improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment. They do not make an animal invulnerable to habitat change or other threats.

Reproduction and life cycle

The queen lays eggs in wax cells. Larvae are fed by workers and develop through complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa and adult. Worker bees and queens receive different diets during development. Successful reproduction depends on suitable breeding habitat, enough food and low levels of disturbance. Young animals often have different needs from adults and may be especially vulnerable during early development.

Conservation and threats

Threats include parasites, pathogens, pesticides, habitat loss and poor nutrition. Honey bees are important managed pollinators, but wild pollinators also need conservation. Conservation status is periodically reassessed and can differ between global and local populations. For current status, consult the latest assessment from the IUCN Red List and relevant national wildlife authorities rather than relying on an undated summary.

How to observe responsibly

Do not disturb nests. Plant diverse pesticide-free flowers and seek professional help if a colony is in an unsafe location. In all wildlife encounters, prioritise the animal's welfare. Do not feed, chase, handle or remove wild animals, and follow protected-area rules.

Learning note: This is an educational species profile, not veterinary, wildlife-handling or field-identification advice. Confirm local regulations and current scientific assessments when using this information for research.
