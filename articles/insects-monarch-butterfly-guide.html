Insects Explained: The Monarch Butterfly Life Cycle

Insects are arthropods with three main body regions: head, thorax and abdomen. Adult insects have six legs, one pair of antennae and, in many groups, wings. Beetles, butterflies, bees, ants, flies, grasshoppers and dragonflies are all insects.

The monarch butterfly

The monarch (Danaus plexippus) is recognised by its orange wings with black veins and white spots. It occurs across parts of the Americas and has become famous for long-distance seasonal migrations in North America. Not every monarch population migrates in the same way.

Four stages of complete metamorphosis

Monarchs develop through four stages: egg, caterpillar (larva), chrysalis (pupa) and adult butterfly. A female lays eggs on milkweed plants. The caterpillar feeds and grows, sheds its skin several times, then forms a chrysalis. Inside, its body is reorganised before an adult emerges.

Why milkweed is essential

Monarch caterpillars feed on milkweed, which provides both food and defensive chemicals. These compounds can make monarchs distasteful to some predators. Adult butterflies feed on nectar from a range of flowering plants, so diverse blooms are important throughout the season.

Migration and navigation

Some North American monarchs travel remarkable distances between breeding areas and overwintering sites. Their migration involves multiple generations in some parts of the annual cycle. Temperature, daylight, wind and inherited orientation behaviours all play roles in movement.

Conservation concerns

Habitat loss, reduced milkweed in some agricultural landscapes, pesticide exposure, extreme weather and climate change can affect monarchs. Conservation approaches include protecting overwintering habitat, planting regionally appropriate milkweed and nectar plants, and reducing unnecessary pesticide use.

Make a pollinator-friendly garden

Choose native flowering plants that bloom at different times, avoid pesticide use where possible, provide sheltered areas and learn which milkweed species are native to your region. Planting non-native milkweed in unsuitable climates can sometimes disrupt local ecological relationships, so local guidance matters.

Insect fact: Butterflies taste with sensors on their feet, helping them assess plants before laying eggs or feeding.
